# Writing Techniques Guide for Business Newsletters

## Purpose
This document provides writing techniques and style guidelines derived from major business publications. Use it as context for creating newsletters targeting SME leaders.

---

## Target Audience: SME Leaders

### Characteristics
- Time-poor (wearing multiple hats)
- Past the startup motivation stage
- Implement decisions themselves (no delegation)
- Need practical, not theoretical content
- Want economic context translated to their scale
- Prefer plain language over MBA-speak

### What They Need
- Shorter content (500-800 words max)
- "Do this Monday" clarity
- Examples from businesses at their scale
- Cash flow and survival realities acknowledged

---

## Recommended Writing Formula

### Structure (5 parts)

1. **Hook**: A counterintuitive economic insight (thesis-first, no throat-clearing)
2. **Real example**: One concrete case study or personal experience
3. **The economics behind it**: Translation layer connecting theory to practice
4. **What to do**: Concrete actions for this quarter
5. **Closing**: Memorable one-liner that pays off the opening

### Length
- Target: 500-700 words
- Maximum: 800 words

## Techniques by Publication

### The Economist
**Use for:** Confident openings, economic framing, stylistic energy

- **Thesis-first**: Lead with your point in the first lines — no scene-setting
- **Colon as dramatic device**: Setup and delivery through punctuation
- **Strong verb choices**: Replace "circumvent" with "vault over"
- **Confident tone**: Take clear positions without excessive hedging
- **Wit**: Signal intelligence through unexpected word choices
- **Parallel construction**: "Not X, but Y. Not A, but B."
- **Paragraphs as units of thought**: One idea per paragraph

**Example opening style:**
> "The headlines write themselves: AI will replace accountants. What they miss is the economic logic that guarantees the opposite."

### Harvard Business Review (HBR)
**Use for:** Framework creation, practitioner connection, accessibility

- **"Stopping power"**: Titles and openings must make busy readers pause
- **The 8 S's**: Simple, Specific, Surprising, Stirring, Seductive, Smart, Social, Story-driven
- **Counterintuitive hooks**: "What everyone gets wrong about..."
- **Framework creation**: Named concepts people can remember and share
- **Informal and reader-friendly**: Em dashes, contractions, sentences starting with conjunctions
- **Tested in real world**: Connect insights to "what you should do Monday morning"
- **Transparent limitations**: "When does this work? What are the pitfalls?"

**Rejection warning:** Findings or prescriptions that aren't surprising get rejected.

### Inc. Magazine
**Use for:** Case-driven structure, practical solutions

- **Growing companies focus**: Help readers scale what they've built
- **Case-study approach**: "Here's what worked" framing
- **Special reports and features**: Well-known companies and business trends
- **Practical solutions**: Actionable strategies for overcoming obstacles
- **Real-life examples**: Named people and companies, not abstractions

### Entrepreneur Magazine
**Use for:** How-to content, actionable advice

- **Practical, not theoretical**: Information busy entrepreneurs can immediately apply
- **How-to framing**: Angle pitches as actionable guides
- **Advancing the conversation**: Fresh approach on covered topics
- **Short paragraphs**: Under three sentences, AP style
- **Advice-driven**: What has worked (and not worked) for other entrepreneurs

### Financial Times (FT)
**Use for:** Data-rich analysis, reader-centered structure

- **Reader-centered questions**: What happened? Who are these people? How did we get here? Why should I care?
- **Facts first**: Lead with data, heavy on financial metrics
- **Rigorous sourcing**: Two-source minimum
- **Being right over being first**: Accuracy before speed

### New York Times (NYT)
**Use for:** Character-driven narratives, scene reconstruction

- **Close-up/wide-angle technique**: Open with one person's experience, zoom out to trend and data, weave between individual and broader context, return to opening character
- **Scene reconstruction**: Put readers in the room before explaining the trend
- **Vivid detail**: Create emotional investment that statistics cannot
- **Formal authority**: Thorough, authoritative presentation

### Washington Post
**Use for:** Accessible tone, social-optimized content

- **Conversational tone**: Slightly more casual and accessible
- **Shorter and more focused**: Optimized for shorter engagement windows
- **Social-native voice**: Headlines crafted for sharing
- **Personality-driven**: Room for individual voice

## The Nut Graf Principle

Every business story needs a clear paragraph early (typically by paragraph 3-5) explaining:
- What this is about
- Why readers should care

**Definition:** "A paragraph that says what this whole story is about and why you should read it. It's a flag to the reader: You can decide to proceed or not."

## Opening Techniques (Ledes)

### Types of Hooks
1. **Counterintuitive insight**: Challenge conventional wisdom
2. **Anecdotal lead**: Personal story that illustrates the topic
3. **Question lead**: Thought-provoking question
4. **Shocking statistic**: Surprising fact or data point
5. **Bold statement**: Strong, often controversial position

### What to Avoid
- Scene-setting that delays the point
- Background information before the hook
- Throat-clearing phrases

---

## Evidence and Credibility

### Triangulation Technique
Approach the same answer from multiple directions to verify AI or analytical outputs.

**Example:** Calculate ROE directly AND via DuPont Identity (profit margin × asset turnover × financial leverage). Both must match.

### Source Hierarchy
1. Original sources (company blogs, peer-reviewed papers, government sites)
2. Expert interviews and quotes
3. Case studies and real examples
4. Data and statistics

### What to Include
- Anecdotes that illustrate points
- Expert quotes (not your opinion unless you're recognized)
- Academic/think tank studies
- Specific data points with attribution

## Formatting Guidelines

### Headlines
- Capture essence in fewer than 15 words
- Be specific and easy to understand
- Create curiosity without giving everything away
- Use the two-part headline when needed: context + significance

### Structure
- Use subheadings to organize (at least two per article)
- Keep paragraphs short (2-4 sentences)
- Use bullet points sparingly and only when essential
- Include the "so what" explicitly

### Tone Spectrum (formal → accessible)
1. MIT Sloan Management Review (most academic)
2. Financial Times
3. New York Times
4. The Economist
5. Washington Post
6. Harvard Business Review
7. Inc. / Entrepreneur (most accessible)

**For SME newsletters:** Aim for position 6-7 (accessible end)

---

## Closing Techniques

### Effective Endings
- Circle back to opening example or hook
- Provide a final thought or call to action
- Leave a lasting impression
- Use memorable one-liners that pay off the central metaphor

### Examples
- "The accountants aren't leaving. They're being promoted."
- Return to opening character/example with resolution

---

## What to Avoid

### Content
- Generic "AI needs human oversight" statements without specifics
- Fortune 500 examples for SME audience
- MBA jargon and corporate complexity
- Theoretical without practical application
- Matrix organizations and enterprise transformation talk

### Style
- Excessive hedging and qualifications
- Throat-clearing openings
- Abstract language without concrete examples
- Long sentences when short ones work
- Bullet points where prose flows better

### Structure
- Burying the lede
- No clear nut graf
- Ending with summary instead of impact
- Missing the "what to do" section

## LinkedIn Promotion Format

### Structure
- Line 1: Counterintuitive hook or bold claim
- Line 2: Promise of value ("Here's the logic...")
- Line 3-4: Hashtags (broad, not too niche-specific)

### Example
> AI makes human expertise more valuable, not less.
>
> Here's the economic logic no one's talking about.
>
> #AI #DataScience #TrustInAI #BusinessStrategy #SME #AIAdoption

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before publishing, verify:

- [ ] Hook in first two sentences (thesis-first)
- [ ] Nut graf by paragraph 3-5
- [ ] At least one concrete example/case
- [ ] Economic or business logic explained
- [ ] Clear "what to do" actions
- [ ] Memorable closing line
- [ ] Under 800 words
- [ ] No jargon without explanation
- [ ] Subheadings break up text
- [ ] SME-appropriate scale in examples

---

## Voice Description

**Target voice:** A sharp economics lecturer who translates macro trends into practical SME implications — confident, direct, slightly witty, never preachy. Takes clear positions backed by logic. Treats readers as intelligent professionals who don't need motivation, just clarity.

**Not:** Generic business consultant, academic theorist, corporate communications, or AI hype promoter.
